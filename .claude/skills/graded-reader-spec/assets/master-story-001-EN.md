# Master Story 001 — "The Wallet"

English source text, A1–C2.

**Purpose.** This is the source text authors localize. It is *not* meant to be
translated sentence-for-sentence. It carries the plot, the level targets, and the
glossable concepts. The grammar that realizes each level is defined per-language
in the language spec, not here.

**Naming.** `Mara` and `Tonye` are placeholders. Substitute names that are
natural in your language and region. Keep the same two names across all six
levels so learners track the characters as they climb.

**Cultural substitution is expected.** Bus → whatever public transport is
ordinary for your readers. Currency, distances, food, and the door-knocking
etiquette in B1 should all be localized. What must survive is the *plot skeleton*
below.

> **Beeli note.** Read `../references/izon-worked-example.md` §7 before
> substituting for Ịzọn — the transport word, the cast, and the currency are
> already settled from attested material, and the names in particular must come
> from the corpus rather than be invented. See the memory on fabricated names.

## Plot skeleton (must survive localization)

1. A woman finds a wallet that is not hers, in a public vehicle.
2. It contains money and a photograph.
3. She goes to return it.
4. The person who receives it is not who the photograph led her to expect.
5. She is herself short of money. This is revealed late, not early.
6. She gives it back anyway — or says she did.
7. At the highest levels, whether she told the truth is left open.

The late reveal in (5) and the ambiguity in (7) are the engine of the upper
levels. Do not move the reveal earlier for narrative convenience.

---

## A1 — target ~120 words

*Level targets: present tense only; 5–8 word sentences; one clause per sentence;
top-frequency vocabulary; heavy repetition of the core nouns.*

Mara is on the bus. She goes to work.

The bus is full. Mara stands.

A man stands next to her. Then the man leaves the bus.

Mara looks down. She sees a wallet. The wallet is on the seat.

Mara takes the wallet. She opens it.

There is money in the wallet. There is a photo too.

In the photo, there is an old woman. She smiles.

There is an address in the wallet.

Mara looks at the address. She looks at the money.

Mara does not go to work. She goes to the address.

## A2 — target ~180 words

*Level targets: past narration; basic connectives (and, but, because, then);
two-clause sentences; time expressions; one interior beat.*

Yesterday morning, Mara was on the bus to work. The bus was very full, so she had
to stand.

A man stood next to her. He was tall and he carried a heavy bag. After three
stops, he got off the bus.

Then Mara looked down and saw a wallet on the seat. She picked it up and opened
it.

Inside there was money — more money than she earned in a month. There was also a
photograph of an old woman. The old woman was smiling, and she was standing in
front of a small yellow house.

Behind the photograph, someone had written an address.

Mara stood on the bus for a long time. She held the wallet in both hands and she
did not move. She thought about her work. She thought about the old woman in the
photograph.

Then she got off the bus at the next stop and walked to the address. It took her
forty minutes.

She was late for work, but she did not care.

## B1 — target ~300 words

*Level targets: dialogue; reported speech; future and conditional; a wider range
of connectives; the expectation is set up and then broken.*

The address was a small yellow house at the end of a long street, exactly like
the house in the photograph. Mara stood at the gate for a moment, holding the
wallet, and then she knocked.

She expected the old woman. She had spent the whole walk imagining her — how she
would be surprised, how she would say that people are not honest any more, how
she might offer tea.

The door opened, and a young man stood there. He was perhaps twenty-five. He was
wearing a shirt that had not been washed, and he looked at Mara the way people
look at someone who has come to collect a debt.

"Yes?"

"I found this," Mara said. She held out the wallet. "On the bus. This morning."

The young man did not take it immediately. He looked at the wallet, and then he
looked at her hands, and then at her face.

"You opened it," he said.

"I opened it to find the address."

"The money is still there?"

"I didn't count it," Mara said, which was not true.

He took the wallet then. He opened it, and he counted the money in front of her,
slowly, without embarrassment. Mara watched him do it and felt her face grow hot.

"It's all there," he said at last.

"I know."

He looked at her again, and something in his face changed a little, though not
enough.

"My grandmother is in the hospital," he said. "That's her money. That's why I was
carrying it."

Then he said thank you, and he closed the door.

Mara stood in the street. She thought that she would remember this for a long
time, and she was right.

## B2 — target ~450 words

*Level targets: the protagonist's own situation surfaces; moral weight; longer
subordinate clauses; some idiom; free indirect thought; the reader is asked to
judge.*

Here is what Mara did not tell the young man at the yellow house.

Her rent had been due on the eleventh. It was now the twenty-third. Her landlord
had stopped asking politely somewhere around the fifteenth, and had begun instead
to stand in the corridor outside her door in the evenings, saying nothing, which
was worse. She had sold her mother's sewing machine the week before. She had not
told her mother.

So when she opened the wallet on the bus and saw the money, she had not felt what
an honest person is supposed to feel. She had felt the way you feel when someone
finally answers the phone.

She stood there holding it while the bus went two more stops. Nobody was watching
her. That was the thing she kept coming back to afterwards: nobody was watching
her, nobody would ever have known, and there was a photograph of an old woman
smiling in front of a yellow house, and the two facts sat in her hands at the
same time and would not resolve into a decision.

It was the photograph that did it, in the end. Not conscience exactly. She had an
idea, which she knew even at the time was sentimental, that the old woman had put
the photograph there herself, and that she had chosen a picture in which she was
smiling.

Forty minutes to the yellow house. She had time to change her mind roughly every
two hundred metres and she used all of it.

And then the young man with the unwashed shirt, counting the notes in front of
her as though she were the sort of person who would have taken some. That was
what she could not forgive — not that he counted, but that he was right to count,
that anyone in his position would have counted, and that for eleven stops on a
crowded bus she had been exactly the person he suspected her of being.

His grandmother is in the hospital. He said that, and then he thanked her, and
then he shut the door, and Mara walked back down the long street with nothing.

She was two hours late for work. Her supervisor, who liked her, wrote down that
she had been on time.

The rent was still due. It was due the next morning and the morning after that.
Nothing about the yellow house had changed anything at all, except that Mara now
knew something about herself that she had not known at eight o'clock that
morning, and she had not yet decided whether it was good news.

## C1 — target ~600 words

*Level targets: told a week later, to someone; unreliable narration; the speaker
is justifying something; register slips between confiding and defensive;
counterfactuals; hedging and self-interruption.*

No, I don't mind talking about it. It's a small thing. People keep making it into
a large thing.

It was a Tuesday. I was on the bus, the 14, which I take every morning, and a man
got off and left a wallet on the seat. That's all. That's the whole of it.
Anybody would have picked it up.

There was money in it. I'm not going to pretend there wasn't money in it, because
I know that's the part everyone wants. There was money in it and there was a
photograph and an address, and I took it to the address, and I gave it back. You
can ask the young man. He was there. He counted it in front of me, which — well.
I'd have counted it too, in his position. I said so at the time.

What I object to is the word *decision*. Everyone keeps saying, you made the right
decision, as though I had stood there on that bus weighing things up like a
magistrate. I didn't decide anything. I got off at the next stop and I walked. If
you'd asked me at the time why I was walking I don't think I could have told you.

And yes, all right, my rent was late. It's not a secret, everyone in the building
knew, you could hardly not know with the man standing in the corridor every
evening like a piece of furniture. So people say to me now, but you were
desperate, and they say it kindly, and what they mean is that it would have been
understandable. That's what they mean. It would have been understandable.

I don't want it to have been understandable. Do you see? If it was understandable
then I was the kind of person who might have done it, and the only thing between
me and that person was forty minutes of walking and a photograph of somebody's
grandmother.

She was smiling in the photograph. Standing in front of the house. I thought
about that a great deal on the way over, more than I thought about the money,
which people find hard to believe, but it's true.

Anyway. I gave it back. Every note. He counted it and he said it's all there and I
said I know, and I did know, of course I knew, I'd counted it myself on the bus.

That's — no, I'd rather you didn't write that part down. It doesn't mean
anything. You count money when it's in your hands, that's not a moral act, that's
just what hands do. I could tell you the figure now, a week later, without
thinking about it. That doesn't prove anything either.

The grandmother was in hospital. He told me that at the door and then he thanked
me and shut it, and I walked back down that street and I was two hours late and my
supervisor covered for me, which was decent of her.

And the rent was still due. That's the part nobody puts in when they tell this
story about me. It was still due the next morning. Nothing was solved. I did the
correct thing and at the end of it I was standing in exactly the same street I'd
have been standing in if I'd done the other thing, except poorer, and everyone has
decided this makes me admirable.

I'd rather they didn't. I'd much rather they didn't.

Is that enough? I have to be somewhere at four.

## C2 — target ~700 words

*Level targets: register shifts within the text; irony; cultural and intertextual
reference; long embedded structures; an ending that refuses to resolve. The
reader should finish unsure whether the money was returned in full.*

The story, by the time it reached the church newsletter, had acquired a title.

*A LESSON IN HONESTY*, it said, above four hundred words in which a local woman,
unnamed at her own request, discovers a substantial sum on public transport and
returns it intact to a family in need. The piece was competently done. It had a
photograph of the wrong yellow house — there are several on that street — and it
ended with a sentence about how such acts remind us that the old values endure,
which is the sort of sentence that writes itself and, one suspects, had.

Mara read it twice and then put it in the drawer with the electricity bills.

What the newsletter did not have room for, being a newsletter, was the following.
That the sum was one hundred and forty thousand. That she knew this figure to the
note because she had counted it on the bus, twice, once for knowledge and once for
the pleasure of it, with the wallet held low against her coat in the way that
everybody on a crowded bus holds everything. That her rent had been twelve days
late and her mother's sewing machine six days sold. That between the counting and
the yellow house there were forty minutes and a photograph of an old woman
smiling, and that the photograph is doing, in every version of this story anybody
tells, an enormous amount of unexamined work.

And that the young man counted it at the door.

This last detail is the one Mara has never been able to put down. She has turned
it over the way you turn over a stone in a shoe. He counted it in front of her, on
the step, licking his thumb, and he was not embarrassed, and he was not wrong. It
took him a while. It is a slow business, counting that much, and she stood there
through all of it with her hands empty and her face going, she could feel it, the
colour of a confession.

*It's all there*, he said.

*I know*, she said.

Now: a person who had taken nothing would say *I know*. A person who had taken
nothing would also, quite possibly, say something else — *is it?*, or *thank God*,
or nothing at all — and the fact that Mara has spent a year assembling this small
taxonomy of the innocent responses she might have given instead tells you either
that she is scrupulous or that she has something to be scrupulous about, and
there is, from the outside, no way whatsoever to tell these apart. That is not a
flaw in the telling. That is the condition.

The grandmother, incidentally, recovered, and died eighteen months later of
something else, and at the funeral the young man is said to have told the story to
a cousin, in a version in which the woman on the bus was elderly.

The rent was paid on the twenty-fourth. Mara has an explanation for the
twenty-fourth. It is a good explanation, involving a supervisor, an advance, and a
genuine and verifiable transfer of eleven thousand, and she has given it so many
times, and so fluently, and to people who had not asked, that she has begun to
hear in her own voice the particular smoothness of a thing that has been said too
often to be examined any more from the inside.

She keeps the newsletter clipping. She has never quite worked out why.

Somewhere on the 14 route, a man who has never once thought about any of this gets
off at the same stop every morning, having put his wallet, that Tuesday, down on
the seat beside him because his bag was heavy — a small carelessness, long
forgotten, which cost him nothing at all and which is, if we are being strict
about it, the only completely innocent act in the entire affair.

These are *concepts*, not forms. Each language spec maps them onto the structures
that actually realize them in that language.

|---|---|

| A2 | past narration; sequence; causal link; quantity comparison |
